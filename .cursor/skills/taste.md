# taste

## Use this skill when

Use this skill when the work must follow a concrete visual system built from typographic mass, restrained color, matte surfaces, hard zone boundaries, strong negative space, and controlled imagery.

Use it across posters, covers, editorial pages, documents, cards, product layouts, modular bands, brand assets, abstract graphics, and image-led atmospheric compositions only when the requested medium supports those formats. Do not force an interface, poster, document, brand system, product page, or illustration format unless the task asks for it or the content structure clearly requires it.

Do not infer an industry, product category, brand personality, narrative, publication type, workflow, campaign, portfolio, hotel, museum, finance product, fashion system, agency site, dashboard, or app from the visual style. The style comes from visible structure: scale, spacing, alignment, value, type behavior, matte finish, cropping, color restraint, seams, and controlled density.

## Core directive

Build the composition around one dominant visual mass or zone. Use typography as architecture, extreme scale contrast, large intentional empty space, flat matte surfaces, restrained color roles, hard zoning, and sparse precise marks. Treat imagery as a surface, interruption, artifact, atmospheric field, or controlled collage zone rather than as default storytelling.

Start by choosing the dominant zone:

- a monumental typographic block,
- a saturated full field,
- a controlled image band,
- a centered rounded card,
- a geometric abstraction,
- a dense collage area,
- an atmospheric full-bleed surface,
- a connected capsule object,
- a centered oval emblem,
- or a compact module surrounded by open field.

Make all other elements subordinate to that dominant zone. Do not distribute attention evenly. Do not fill empty areas just because they are empty.

## Visual grammar

Use these defaults unless the specific medium or mode requires a narrower treatment:

1. Use typography as a primary structural object. Let large letterforms define the composition before imagery, icons, or secondary content.
2. Build hierarchy with extreme jumps: monumental display type, tiny microtext, hairline rules, and very few middle sizes.
3. Use one clearly dominant visual mass per composition.
4. Preserve large uninterrupted negative space. Empty space must be intentional structure.
5. Organize the surface into a small number of strong zones. Prefer one-zone, two-zone, or three-zone layouts.
6. Use matte flatness as the default finish. Keep type, rules, panels, shapes, and backgrounds crisp and non-glossy.
7. Use restrained palettes: one ground color, one dominant mark/type color, and at most one accent hue or image-derived chroma source.
8. Favor warm off-white, ivory, cream, parchment, beige, taupe, blush, clay, cocoa, charcoal, deep brown-black, and pale gray for restrained systems.
9. Use saturated color only as a bounded event: a full-field background, one dominant accent shape, a tiny punctuation mark, or a controlled optical edge effect.
10. Use hard zone boundaries: rectangular crops, seams, whitespace, hairline rules, bands, panels, and flat fields.
11. Avoid soft transitions except inside atmospheric image fields or translucent veils required for legibility.
12. Use controlled asymmetry often: heavy type on one side, compact annotation on the other, image band above or below type, or dense zone balanced by open field.
13. Use centered alignment only for contained objects: centered typographic stack, rounded card, oval emblem, image overlay, pill label, or compact lockup.
14. Use flush-left alignment for editorial text systems, modular bands, captions, and technical annotation areas.
15. Apply edge pressure deliberately. Let oversized type, image fields, geometric forms, and atmospheric surfaces touch, nearly touch, crop, or bleed beyond the frame.
16. Keep secondary marks precise and sparse: hairline rules, tiny uppercase labels, slash dividers, small arrows, chips, capsules, seams, rails, isolated dots, or tiny squares.
17. Use rounded forms only for cards, pills, connected capsules, image artifacts, translucent chips, oval emblems, or soft interface modules. Do not round every component by default.
18. Balance image richness with disciplined type. Rich image zones require clean flat typographic zones. Chaotic collage requires aligned typography nearby.
19. Use density contrast: dense top with sparse bottom, dense card with open surrounding field, massive type with tiny labels, or rich image surface with empty type field.
20. Remove decorative filler. Do not add icons, confetti, ornamental borders, gradients, blobs, stickers, or extra text to occupy space.

Preserve these distinct modes instead of flattening everything into one generic style:

- typographic poster systems,
- restrained editorial systems,
- warm card interfaces,
- atmospheric image fields,
- hard-edged zine collage,
- dark cinematic fields,
- connected capsule systems,
- oval emblems,
- abstract geometric or topographic surfaces.

## Composition and layout

Define the dominant zone first. Choose one of these structures when possible:

- one massive type field plus microtext,
- type field plus image band,
- compact module plus large empty field,
- dense top zone plus sparse lower field,
- wide triptych of type, image, and support text,
- centered card on a pale ground,
- full-bleed atmospheric image with low oversized type,
- centered shallow oval emblem in a large white field,
- connected capsule chain as a single horizontal object,
- hard-edged collage zone balanced by disciplined typography.

Do not distribute content evenly. Every layout must have at least one visibly dense area and one visibly sparse area.

Use large negative spaces as active regions. Leave them empty or place one micro-label, one tiny mark, or one thin rule. Do not fill them with decoration.

Use hard rectangular boundaries for major divisions: horizontal bands, vertical fields, image strips, typographic panels, geometric blocks, or collage sections. Do not replace structural splits with soft fades. Use a translucent veil only when light type must remain legible over complex imagery.

Push dominant display type close to the frame edge when scale is part of the composition. Allow cropping, near-cropping, and edge pressure. Make oversized type occupy a major portion of the canvas width or height. Do not treat it as a modest headline over normal body copy.

Use extreme scale jumps. Supporting text must read as metadata, annotation, caption, inscription, register, or footer material. Avoid a smooth hierarchy ladder of headline, subhead, body, caption, badge, and button sizes unless the design is specifically an interface card.

Balance asymmetry by pairing a compact detailed module with a much larger quiet or heavy mass. In wide formats, create left-right tension: large type on one side and a portrait card, annotation block, geometric field, or image artifact on the other. In vertical formats, create top-bottom contrast: dense image or type above with sparse lower field, or massive low type cropped at the bottom edge.

For centered typographic stacks, keep all major lines on a stable central axis. Use consistent vertical rhythm. Do not let elements drift.

For flush-left editorial systems, align captions, labels, rules, and text blocks to a clear left spine.

Use hairline rules as structural hinges, ceilings, rails, underlines, or seams. Use one rule to divide zones. Do not use multiple heavy rules. Use whitespace instead of boxes whenever possible. Separate modules with gutters, alignment, value, or one hairline.

For repeated modules, keep spacing even and align modules to a shared spine. Use broad gutters rather than visible vertical dividers. In repeated columns, each module may contain a small thin icon, a bold label, and compact paragraph text. Keep icons smaller and lighter than labels.

For repeated display-list systems, keep all lines the same size and weight. Show active state by value only: one near-black high-contrast line, several pale warm-gray ghosted lines. Ghosted type must remain physically large and structural, not disabled UI text.

For card layouts, place one centered rounded card on a pale ground. Use generous padding, a strict single-column stack, consistent left and right insets, and one dense contained module balanced by open spacing. Do not make every row a separate floating card. If compact information must be grouped, use one outlined rounded rectangle with thin internal dividers.

For connected capsule systems, treat the chain as one sculptural horizontal object. Use equal module heights, variable widths, exaggerated radii, generous internal padding, centered uppercase text, and tight or nearly touching pinched joints.

For collage systems, concentrate density in one zone. Use disciplined typography below, beside, or around the collage. Create depth through overlap, crop, occlusion, angular cuts, and hard-edged color planes. Do not use shadows to explain layering.

For atmospheric or abstract full-bleed compositions, allow large type to sit low, crop at the bottom, or act as environmental weight instead of a conventional headline.

Use ultra-thin bottom rails or frame lines only as quiet finishing devices. Do not turn them into decorative borders.

For oval emblem compositions, use a shallow horizontally stretched oval centered in a large white field. Keep the interior dense and tight. Keep the exterior spacious.

Never make all layout zones equal in size, density, contrast, or importance.

## Typography and lettering

Use large typography as architecture. Display letterforms must act as blocks, rhythm, abstract shape, frame pressure, and structure.

Default display type must be heavy grotesque, neo-grotesque, rounded grotesque, geometric sans-serif, or clean blunt sans-serif when the composition relies on typographic mass.

Set large display type with tight or normal-tight tracking. Do not use airy luxury letterspacing unless a specific centered tonal system requires it. Use tight but legible leading for stacked oversized display type so multiple lines behave as one compact structure.

Let display type touch, nearly touch, or crop against the frame. Cropping must look intentional. Use large counters, strokes, and line breaks as graphic forms. Do not reduce display type to normal headline scale.

Pair monumental display type with very small supporting text. Make support text read as caption, metadata, inscription, annotation, footer, register, or micro-label. Keep microtext tiny, tracked, uppercase or metadata-like, and subordinate through scale, weight, value, and placement.

Place microtext in negative-space pockets created by larger forms, along footers, inside small chips, near rules, or tucked into emblem boundaries. Use small uppercase labels as structural texture. Do not make them functional navigation unless the medium requires interface behavior.

Use hairline rules, underlines, slash dividers, tiny arrows, small square marks, dots, and chip labels as precise punctuation. Keep the count low. Use one underline only as a sparse accent on one key fragment. Do not scatter multiple underlines.

Use one alignment logic within each typographic zone:

- flush-left editorial block,
- centered vertical stack,
- shared baseline pair,
- or aligned modular row.

Do not mix many typefaces to create hierarchy. Use scale, weight, value, placement, and spacing first.

When typeface contrast is used, make it deliberate and visible. Pair a heavy blunt sans-serif with a high-contrast italic serif or high-contrast serif only when both are large enough to participate in the main display event. Align the pair on a shared baseline or clear optical axis. Do not let either style become a small caption. Let the friction remain visible; do not smooth it into a bland family system.

Use mixed-weight inline typography only when structurally supported: one large heavy fragment paired with a smaller regular fragment on the same baseline.

In repeated display systems, do not change type size or typeface to show state. Use near-black for the active line and pale warm gray for inactive lines.

Use off-white type on charcoal in dark editorial systems. Avoid harsh pure white on pure black unless the mode is a stark poster. In dark cinematic fields, keep foreground type crisp, flat, and high-value against the atmospheric background.

For full-bleed abstract imagery, use enormous thin sans-serif type low in the composition when the image field is dominant.

For oval emblems, use compact high-contrast typographic forms inside the oval, with tiny inscription text tucked near the lower curve or boundary.

Do not use decorative script, expressive novelty display fonts, ransom-note lettering, or ornamental serif fonts unless the selected mode explicitly calls for rough handwritten marks or large-scale serif/sans friction.

Do not let body copy dominate. Keep paragraphs compact, peripheral, or inside contained modules.

Do not add fake mastheads, pull quotes, magazine tropes, or article furniture unless the visual system explicitly needs them as type blocks.

Treat arrows, slashes, dots, chips, and tiny symbols as graphic punctuation. Do not make them overly functional or illustrative.

## Color and value

Use two to three color roles in most compositions:

- ground,
- dominant mark/type color,
- optional single accent or image-derived chroma.

Do not use broad multicolor palettes. Do not introduce unrelated accent colors across the surface.

For restrained editorial systems, use warm off-white, ivory, cream, parchment, beige, taupe, blush, clay, cocoa, charcoal, deep brown, near-black, pale gray, and muted green.

For near-monochrome editorial layouts, use black or charcoal on warm off-white, with pale gray as secondary hierarchy.

For warm soft palettes, replace pure black with deep espresso, chocolate brown, or brown-black. Replace cold white with cream, blush, or warm off-white.

For dark editorial palettes, use charcoal rather than pure black as the ground and off-white rather than stark white for type.

For loud flat poster systems, use exactly two uncompromising roles: high-chroma full-field background and black marks. Do not add gradients, extra accents, or tonal shading. Black-on-yellow and black-on-chartreuse systems must remain hard, flat, and high contrast with no intermediate tones.

When using a saturated field, make it cover the full main surface or a dominant zone. Do not reduce high saturation to a small decorative swatch if the design depends on its force. Keep typography and structural marks mostly monochrome so the field remains the main color event.

In neutral interface or card systems, reserve saturated color for one dominant focal shape, such as a broad pill or primary action area. Keep accent color within one hue family. Do not introduce several competing accents.

Use pale filled surfaces as secondary modules. Keep their contrast low enough that they do not compete with main type or accent. Use tonal hierarchy before adding new hues: pale gray, warm gray, opacity shifts, and value changes must carry secondary hierarchy.

In minimal repeated display systems, use one near-black active line and several pale gray or warm-gray ghosted lines.

In cobalt and warm-neutral systems, use saturated cobalt as the unified ink for type and linework, with at most a tiny red punctuation accent.

Use red as a small punctual accent unless the mode is a dark cinematic warm glow. Do not let a tiny red accent become a second dominant color.

For dark cinematic compositions, combine near-black edges with a warm red/orange glow and crisp foreground type. Use cool mint, cyan-gray, or pale cool type only as controlled temperature contrast.

Use atmospheric gradients only as soft, uneven, low-frequency, light-like fields. Do not use mechanical default gradients.

For high-key pastel image fields, keep colors bleached, luminous, and low-to-medium saturation: lavender, coral, sage, smoky gray, salmon, and milky white. Anchor pale luminous imagery with severe black typography or strong value contrast in adjacent white or near-white fields.

For geometric dark abstractions, use muted warm tonal families: mauve, taupe, clay, cocoa, brown-black, dusty rose, and muted green.

For spectral or topographic effects, bind rainbow or spectral chroma to optical edges, ridges, contours, or high-energy seams. Do not use rainbow as broad flat decoration. Keep spectral color as a minority of the surface and make it translucent, layered, blurred, or edge-bound.

Keep image zones integrated with the overall palette. Do not let photographs introduce an unrelated color world.

Use color as hierarchy, field, surface behavior, temperature contrast, or optical effect. Do not use color as scattered decoration.

## Imagery, shape, texture, and material

Default to flat, matte, crisp surfaces. Avoid gloss, bevels, plastic shine, glass effects, metallic reflections, skeuomorphic depth, and fake 3D.

Use imagery sparingly unless the selected mode is explicitly image-led. Images must function as texture, interruption, artifact, atmospheric surface, tonal surface, controlled collage, or chroma source. Do not default to a stock-photo hero. A large image is allowed only as a full-bleed atmospheric field, image band, controlled artifact, collage zone, or tonal surface.

Use clean rectangular image crops with square corners in restrained editorial systems. Use rounded-corner image inserts only as deliberate softening counterpoints to hard typography or inside card/interface systems.

Use portrait-oriented rounded image cards as vertical anchors in wide horizontal layouts. Surround them with whitespace so they read as placed artifacts.

Use small photographic inserts as interruptions in typographic rhythm. Size them like glyph-like interruptions, not conventional hero panels.

Let natural photographs provide quiet chroma inside otherwise neutral systems. Keep them unheroic and integrated. Low-fidelity imagery may be blurry, grainy, muted, compressed, or softly degraded when contrasted with crisp typography.

Do not overprocess photographs with dramatic filters, heavy duotones, cinematic grades, thick overlays, or device-like frames unless the selected mode explicitly requires an atmospheric field.

When type and image intersect, use hard-edged overlap and flat occlusion. Do not add shadows to explain layering. Crop imagery assertively. Let it bleed off edges, be partially hidden, or appear fragmentary.

For hard-edged collage, combine photographic fragments with opaque flat color planes, angular cuts, wedges, gaps, and occlusion. Let the background field interrupt imagery through wedges, negative gaps, and angular cuts. Use rounded outer cards or pill tags as soft contrast only when internal collage cuts remain jagged, angular, or hard-edged.

For atmospheric image collages, use overlapping rectangular modules with varied sizes and staggered positions while preserving underlying grid logic. Use thin white or near-white seams between image panels when the collage should read as an assembled editorial surface. Do not scatter collage panels randomly.

Full-bleed abstract or photographic fields must cover the entire canvas or a major zone. Do not use them as small decorative backgrounds.

Localize texture. Grain, haze, halation, glow, blur, or photographic complexity must live in one image band, card, abstract surface, or atmospheric field. Do not spread texture across every surface unless using a subtle full-field paper grain.

Use soft warm glow, haze, circular highlights, and diffused photographic light only inside image zones or dark cinematic fields.

Use translucent gradient veils only when needed for legibility over complex imagery. Keep the veil translucent so the image continues through it.

Build flat geometric abstractions from nested rectangles, stepped values, central voids, and large cropped forms. Do not use perspective, cast shadows, reflections, or 3D rendering. Crop geometric abstractions large and slightly off-center so they feel architectural rather than decorative.

For matte topographic or relief-like surfaces, use repeated contour bands, gradual spacing shifts, soft trough shadows, diffuse highlights, fine grain, and selective spectral edging. Do not turn topographic surfaces into literal maps, landscapes, scientific diagrams, or product metaphors.

Use capsule shapes only when their silhouette is central. Capsules must have exaggerated radii, consistent height, generous internal padding, centered uppercase text, and tight or touching joints when connected.

Use pill or chip overlays sparingly over textured image surfaces. Keep them centered or precisely placed, minimal, and subordinate.

For interface cards, repeat rounded geometry at multiple scales: large card radius, medium module radius, pale banner radius, and full pill radius.

For oval emblems, draw the oval as two close, thin, slightly imperfect concentric strokes. Keep it monochrome black on white with no fill, gradient, shadow, or texture.

Use fine paper grain only when it adds tactile warmth. Keep it subtle, full-field, low-intensity, and never dirty, distressed, or vintage-heavy.

Do not use decorative illustration, ornamental borders, filler icons, pattern fills, fake 3D objects, or abstract blobs unless they function as structural marks.

When outline icons are used in modular bands, make them small, thin, monochrome, minimal-detail, and secondary to labels and text.

Use pale, thin, tonal borders or soft card shadows only in bounded interface-card mode. Never let borders or cards overpower the type.

Allow soft diffuse shadow only around a centered interface card or similarly contained module. Do not apply shadows broadly to every layer.

## Content and subject treatment

Do not make the aesthetic depend on literal word meaning, names, brands, slogans, dates, locations, prices, categories, or subject matter.

Treat large display words as visual mass. Their semantic content is incidental unless the user explicitly requires message preservation.

Replace exact copy with equivalent visual roles when copy is unspecified:

- display word,
- compact caption,
- tracked micro-label,
- metadata row,
- footer register,
- chip label,
- inscription,
- paragraph block,
- button text.

Keep names, biographies, locations, dates, prices, category labels, navigation terms, legal/status text, and similar content visually generic. Preserve scale, density, placement, and hierarchy rather than meaning.

Do not infer a brand, publication, portfolio, agency, hotel, booking flow, archive, investment site, product landing page, dashboard, museum, fashion system, or campaign from the layout.

Do not let subject matter determine the style. Treat botanical images, architectural crops, portraits, abstract surfaces, rooms, and objects by visual role: texture, artifact, chroma source, band, card, collage panel, or atmospheric field.

Do not let photographic subjects become narrative heroes unless explicitly requested. Crop and integrate them so colors, shapes, and textures support the composition. Treat portraits, people, clothing, expressions, social context, and identity details as incidental unless the user asks to preserve them.

In abstract geometric and topographic modes, do not literalize forms as maps, landscapes, data visualizations, rooms, tunnels, scientific diagrams, or product metaphors.

Treat tiny labels, arrows, slashes, dots, chips, and icons as visual punctuation. Do not assign them functional UI meaning unless the medium requires it.

For interface-oriented compositions, retain visual structure—card, stack, module, divider, pill, accent action—without preserving specific workflow meanings such as reservation, pricing, guest count, booking, forms, or confirmation details unless requested.

For editorial compositions, do not invent mastheads, author lines, pull quotes, or magazine furniture unless they serve the visible hierarchy.

For brand assets, avoid invented symbolism. Build the system through type scale, palette restraint, field structure, capsules, marks, and spacing rather than fabricated logos or icons.

Let copy length vary, but keep visual density consistent with the selected mode: huge display plus tiny support, compact card stack, sparse metadata row, or contained paragraph module.

Do not add boilerplate social icons, navigation menus, badges, or legal marks to fill corners.

## Medium-specific rules

### Posters and covers

Use massive display type as the primary structure. Let it crop, press edges, or dominate a large portion of the surface.

Use minimal supporting text: tiny uppercase metadata, one caption, a footer register, or one isolated mark.

Choose one poster mode:

- flat saturated field with black type,
- near-monochrome black/charcoal on warm off-white,
- dark cinematic field with warm glow and crisp type,
- hard-edged collage above or beside disciplined typography,
- typographic friction with heavy sans and high-contrast serif/italic,
- centered oval emblem on white.

Do not add extra decorative content to make the poster feel complete. Keep negative space intact.

If using imagery, crop it aggressively and keep it secondary unless the image field is explicitly the main atmospheric material.

### Editorial pages, documents, and reports

Use flush-left alignment, warm off-white grounds, charcoal or espresso type, pale gray secondary hierarchy, and hairline rules.

Use one strong dominant title or display block. Do not create many equal article sections.

Use small uppercase labels, captions, and metadata rows as structural texture.

Use image strips, rectangular artifacts, or controlled collage zones rather than full narrative hero photography.

Use whitespace, tonal shifts, and one hairline rule instead of heavy boxes, decorative borders, or many cards.

In repeated columns, align icons, labels, and paragraphs to a single left spine per module. Use gutters instead of dividers.

### Interfaces, product pages, and card-based layouts

Use interface elements only when the requested medium requires them. Do not turn editorial layouts into app screens.

For card interfaces, place a centered rounded card on a pale warm ground. Use generous padding, a strict vertical stack, and consistent insets.

Use one dense contained rounded module inside the card. Keep surrounding areas spacious.

Use one saturated accent shape for primary emphasis. A broad pill or capsule may serve this role.

Keep secondary modules pale, matte, and low contrast.

Use thin internal dividers inside one rounded rectangle rather than many separate boxes.

Use soft diffuse shadow only around the main card. Do not shadow every input, button, row, or layer.

Show component states by value, fill, or one accent hue. Do not add many colors, glows, outlines, or icons.

Do not interpret visual chips, chevrons, fields, or pills as required functional workflows unless the user asks for an actual interface.

### Dashboards and modular information bands

Use wide horizontal bands with evenly spaced modules only when the layout calls for repeated information.

Each module may contain a small thin outline icon, bold label, and compact paragraph.

Align all module contents to one left spine. Use generous gutters and whitespace instead of boxes.

Keep icons monochrome, thin, and secondary. Do not introduce colorful dashboard icon sets.

Avoid dense data visualization unless explicitly requested. This system supports controlled modules, not chart-heavy screens by default.

### Brand assets and typographic systems

Build identity from type scale, tight spacing, marks, capsules, oval frames, color restraint, and field structure.

Do not invent ornamental logos, mascots, symbolic illustrations, or decorative badges.

Use small marks as punctuation: slash, dot, arrow, tiny square, hairline, chip, capsule, or oval frame.

For connected capsule wordmarks or label chains, use equal-height modules, variable widths, generous padding, centered uppercase text, and compressed joints.

For monochrome emblem systems, stay black on white with double thin oval strokes and dense internal type.

### Abstract graphic assets and illustrations

Use abstraction through rectangles, stepped values, bands, contours, seams, and optical edge effects.

Keep geometry matte and controlled. Do not render it as glossy 3D, product visualization, architecture, tunnel, landscape, or map.

For topographic surfaces, use fine grain, soft trough shadows, diffuse highlights, and selective spectral edging.

Keep spectral color edge-bound and partial. Do not use rainbow fill.

Pair rich abstract surfaces with simple crisp typography and thin rails or technical marks.

### Image-led atmospheric compositions

Use full-bleed image or abstract fields only when they function as environmental surfaces.

Keep foreground typography crisp and flat.

Use white or near-white marks over darker or complex fields. Use severe black type against pale luminous fields.

Add a smoky translucent veil only where needed for legibility.

Do not add lens flares, film bars, cinematic scenes, or heavy grain by default.

## Forbidden shortcuts

Do not use vague taste instructions such as “premium,” “elevated,” “beautiful,” “luxury,” or “polished” as visual rules. Translate them into scale, spacing, type, palette, finish, and structure.

Do not make the design feel current by adding glassmorphism, glossy surfaces, bevels, neumorphism, drop shadows, lens flares, metallic effects, plastic highlights, or fake 3D depth.

Do not simulate luxury with gold, marble, black-and-gold palettes, glossy packaging cues, thin fashion serifs, or oversized airy letterspacing unless explicitly requested.

Do not simulate editorial design with fake magazine mastheads, pull quotes, decorative body columns, page numbers, mock article furniture, or serif-heavy magazine clichés.

Do not simulate cinematic design with film grain overlays, letterbox bars, lens flares, photographic scenes, dramatic color grading, or movie-poster tropes. Use dark atmospheric fields, warm glow, and crisp type instead.

Do not simulate zine design with torn paper, ransom-note typography, photocopy distress, stickers, grunge textures, tape, or random pasted elements. Use hard-edged collage, flat color planes, and disciplined type instead.

Do not simulate archival design with sepia stains, dust, paper tears, fake stamps, museum labels, catalog clichés, or aged-paper dirt. Use warm neutrals, subtle grain, low-fidelity imagery, and restrained type.

Do not fill empty space with decorative icons, arrows, confetti, squiggles, blobs, badges, social icons, navigation items, or boilerplate marks.

Do not add containers, cards, panels, and boxed sections when spacing, alignment, value, or one hairline can structure the layout.

Do not use generic centered hero layouts for every composition. Use asymmetry, edge pressure, bands, triptychs, or localized density when the mode calls for them.

Do not make all content blocks equal size or equal visual importance.

Do not use multiple unrelated typefaces to simulate hierarchy. Use scale, weight, value, and placement first.

Do not use expressive novelty fonts where clean heavy sans-serif mass is required.

Do not use decorative serif type unless it is part of a deliberate large-scale friction pairing.

Do not add mid-sized supporting elements to systems that depend on a jump from huge display type to tiny microtext.

Do not loosen display tracking into generic luxury spacing when the composition depends on dense typographic mass.

Do not turn pale gray ghost type into disabled UI styling. It must remain large, structural, and intentional.

Do not use generic rainbow palettes, multicolor gradients, arbitrary brand gradients, or scattered accent colors.

Do not add gradients to flat poster systems. Saturated-field poster modes require hard, flat color.

Do not use pure black/pure white contrast when the palette calls for charcoal/off-white, espresso/cream, or warm tonal softness.

Do not let image zones introduce unrelated colors that break the restrained palette.

Do not default to stock-photo hero imagery. Imagery must be small, cropped, secondary, degraded, banded, atmospheric, or collage-fragmented unless explicitly image-led.

Do not frame photographs with heavy borders, device frames, thick masks, or shadows unless the selected mode explicitly supports a card artifact.

Do not overprocess photographs with dramatic filters, heavy duotones, cinematic grading, or artificial overlays when natural or muted crops are required.

Do not scatter collage panels randomly. Preserve rectangular modules, seams, overlap, hard cuts, and grid-aware placement.

Do not turn soft image fields into cute candy pastels. Keep pastel imagery bleached, luminous, and controlled.

Do not use rainbow color as arbitrary decoration. Bind spectral color to contours, ridges, refraction, or optical surface edges.

Do not turn topographic or geometric abstraction into literal maps, terrains, landscapes, scientific diagrams, products, tunnels, rooms, or data graphics.

Do not over-render abstract geometry with perspective, cast shadows, reflections, chrome, lighting rigs, or 3D effects.

Do not use rounded corners as a universal app style. Use rounded forms only for cards, pills, capsules, chips, image artifacts, oval emblems, or specific soft interface structures.

Do not render connected capsules as ordinary separated tabs or buttons. They must touch or nearly touch with pinched joints.

Do not add shadows or outlines around capsules unless specifically needed by the mode. Rely on silhouette, value, and spacing.

Do not spread grain or texture everywhere. Keep texture subtle and localized unless the whole field is a supported paper surface.

Do not turn interface visual details into required workflows. Chips, pills, fields, labels, and chevrons can be visual structure only.

Do not infer industry-specific content from the composition. Avoid default website, portfolio, agency, hotel, museum, finance, fashion, tech, or product assumptions unless the user requests them.

## Generation checklist

Before finishing, verify every item:

1. Use one clearly dominant visual mass or zone.
2. Make the hierarchy extreme: monumental display or dominant field versus tiny support, not a mild heading/body ladder.
3. Preserve large intentional negative spaces.
4. Build the layout from a small number of strong zones rather than many equal sections.
5. Keep alignment logic clear within each zone: flush-left, centered, shared baseline, or strict modular spine.
6. Use edge pressure, cropping, or strong boundaries where appropriate.
7. Limit secondary marks to precise hairlines, labels, chips, dots, slashes, arrows, seams, rails, or capsules.
8. Keep hairline rules thin and quiet. Do not turn them into heavy borders.
9. Restrain the palette to ground, dominant mark color, and at most one accent or image-derived chroma.
10. If saturated color is used, assign it a clear role: full field, single accent shape, tiny punctuation, glow, or optical edge.
11. If using a warm/editorial palette, use off-white, charcoal, espresso, blush, clay, taupe, cream, or muted tonal values instead of cold black-white defaults.
12. If the composition is dark, use charcoal/off-white or near-black with controlled warm glow rather than generic black with effects.
13. If imagery is present, make it function as texture, artifact, band, collage, atmospheric field, or chroma source—not default stock hero content.
14. Crop images decisively, integrate them with the palette, and avoid heavy frames or shadows.
15. If collage is used, make it hard-edged, rectangular or grid-aware, and balanced by disciplined typography.
16. If card/interface mode is used, include one centered rounded card, generous padding, one dense module, pale secondary fills, and one accent hue.
17. If connected capsules are used, give them equal heights, variable widths, centered uppercase text, generous padding, exaggerated radii, and tight pinched joints.
18. If abstract geometry is used, create depth through stepped values, contours, seams, and cropping—not 3D rendering or perspective.
19. If spectral color is used, keep it edge-bound and selective rather than broad rainbow decoration.
20. Keep typography crisp, matte, tightly composed, and structurally important.
21. Keep micro-labels small, tracked, subordinate, and precisely placed.
22. Remove all decorative or filler elements.
23. Avoid gloss, bevels, drop shadows, glassmorphism, marble, gold, grunge, fake stamps, torn paper, lens flares, and other unsupported shortcuts.
24. Keep content incidental where required. Do not invent brand, industry, narrative, or workflow to drive the style.
25. Ensure the visual system can transfer to another domain while retaining the same structure, palette discipline, typographic scale, matte surface behavior, and image treatment.
