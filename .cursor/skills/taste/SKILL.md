# taste

## Use this skill when

Use this skill to produce poster-like, cover-like, editorial, landing page, brand, document, interface, collage, or abstract compositions that must behave as one unified graphic field with radical scale jumps, restrained palettes, crisp typography, sparse notation, and disciplined negative space.

Do not use this skill to create conventional dashboard layouts, uniform card grids, decorative luxury styling, glossy technology screens, narrative stock-photo hero designs, or ornament-heavy compositions unless the user explicitly requests those structures and you still apply the constraints below.

## Core directive

Treat the canvas as a single graphic field. Build the design around one dominant visual mass: oversized type, a cropped image field, a localized collage cluster, a rounded-panel pair, a packed emblem, an atmospheric abstract surface, or one expressive mark. Make hierarchy through extreme scale contrast, not through many medium-sized headings. Preserve large quiet fields. Concentrate density in a few zones. Use small marks as precise spatial notation. Keep surfaces flat, matte, crisp, and controlled.

Choose one main structure and execute it decisively. Do not combine every supported mode in one composition.

## Visual grammar

1. Use typography as architecture, mass, rhythm, or cropped geometry. Do not treat large type as a normal caption.
2. Use one monumental register, one tiny metadata register, and optionally one medium image or panel register.
3. Keep visible type sizes to 2–4 levels. Avoid middle-sized filler headings.
4. Preserve large uninterrupted background fields. Empty space must separate, amplify, counterbalance, or protect content.
5. Use asymmetrical balance by default. Counterweight a massive type block, image field, or expressive mark with small edge marks, microtype, a dense corner, or a compact counter-zone.
6. Concentrate density into one collage band, one central cluster, one image card, one emblem interior, one rounded-panel system, or one atmospheric field.
7. Leave other zones quiet or nearly empty.
8. Let oversized type or imagery crop off the canvas when the composition should read as a slice of a larger surface.
9. Keep most layouts edge-aware. Align, press, crop, or hover elements near the frame edge.
10. Use flat matte surfaces by default. Use soft atmospheric bloom only when the entire field or image zone calls for it.
11. Use a narrow palette: one ground, one primary mark color, and at most one accent or muted secondary family.
12. Use value contrast as the main hierarchy device.
13. Use dots, slashes, hairlines, tiny squares, pills, stamps, seals, micro-labels, corner marks, and dotted rules only to anchor, calibrate, punctuate, separate, or pace the layout.
14. Make microtype tiny, compact, tracked, uppercase or metadata-like. Place it near edges, seams, corners, lower baselines, panel footers, or gaps.
15. Keep vector typography, rules, and marks crisp against softer image fields, blurred atmospheres, grain, or photographic texture.
16. Allow one expressive interruption when useful: a high-contrast serif, italic, script, calligraphic mark, irregular illustration, organic cutout, or singular graphic symbol. Keep it singular and controlled.

## Composition and layout

1. Select one dominant compositional logic:
  - oversized type field plus small annotations;
  - flat type field paired with photographic or abstract image field;
  - dense collage band paired with spacious type zone;
  - sparse central type surrounded by irregular image fragments;
  - diptych of two rounded panels;
  - packed typographic emblem inside an otherwise empty field;
  - full-bleed atmospheric abstract field with perimeter type.
2. Do not mix all layout modes. Use one main structure per composition.
3. In sparse editorial layouts, keep roughly 60–80% of the canvas open.
4. In high-density layouts, localize density in one chosen zone.
5. Use negative space to separate zones, amplify scale, protect a headline, or create diagonal tension.
6. Avoid even distribution. Use clusters, voids, edge anchors, off-center masses, and counterweights.
7. Use flush-left alignment by default for disciplined editorial structures.
8. Use centered alignment only for an emblem, central headline anchor, diptych panel system, or protected typographic center.
9. When stacking oversized display lines, use staggered horizontal starts. A descending cascade is allowed.
10. Use tight leading in large multi-line display stacks. Gaps between display lines must be visibly smaller than the letter height.
11. Let architectural type nearly touch, press against, or crop beyond at least one frame or container edge.
12. Use hard cropping to imply a larger typographic, photographic, or geometric surface beyond the canvas.
13. Keep generous outer margins unless full-bleed imagery or deliberately cropped type is the structural move.
14. Put tiny marks, labels, rotated type, rails, captions, and calibration marks around the perimeter, near seams, or at corners.
15. Use diagonal tension in sparse layouts: balance an upper-left type mass with a lower-right micro-cluster, or counter central type with cropped edge fragments.
16. For two-zone layouts, give each register a clear area: half-canvas, strong horizontal band, or strong vertical band. Keep the boundary crisp and planar.
17. For broad horizontal banding, separate strata clearly: large type zone, central cluster or image object, second large type zone, and tiny support text.
18. For collage bands, use hard-edged rectangular or angular modules with varied widths and heights. Organize them through seams, cuts, masks, and occlusion. Do not use decorative frames.
19. For image constellations, place photographic fragments irregularly around the dominant type. Do not use a uniform masonry grid.
20. For diptychs, use two large rounded panels of near-equal size, separated by a clean gutter and surrounded by generous margins.
21. For rounded panel systems, stage one floating image or content tile inside each large panel and reserve a lower flat color-field floor for tiny footer text.
22. For emblem layouts, pack the interior tightly inside a thin contour or outline system and leave the exterior field mostly empty.
23. Use hairline rules as datums. A single horizontal rule may ground a wide composition. A single vertical hairline may separate a compact text column from a dominant type mass.
24. Use bottom or top label rows only as tiny rhythmic structural bands.
25. Keep repeated marks sparse. Repetition must create calibration, pacing, or perimeter rhythm, not wallpaper.
26. Use split footer logic when relevant: tiny labels at left/right or outer thirds with a central gap.
27. Create grouping with spacing, alignment, scale, and position before adding boxes or dividers.
28. Do not default to hero, subhead, buttons, feature cards, testimonials, and footer.

## Typography and lettering

1. Make display type a visual object. It may occupy one-third to one-half of the canvas height in wide compositions, most of the width in poster/card formats, or behave as a cropped background mass.
2. Maintain at least an 8:1 height gap between display type and microtype. In strongly typographic layouts, use 15:1 or greater.
3. Use only these typographic registers unless the user’s medium requires otherwise:
  - dominant display;
  - tiny support or metadata;
  - optional contrasting expressive voice;
  - optional medium label or panel text.
4. Do not introduce many intermediate text sizes.
5. Use heavy grotesque or neo-grotesque sans-serif display type for blunt graphic mass.
6. Use lightweight or regular clean sans-serif display type for enormous pale or white lettering over luminous abstract imagery or for an airy central anchor.
7. Use monumental serif display type for warm editorial structures on cream, blush, or paper-like grounds.
8. Use high-contrast italic serif, script, or calligraphic forms only as a deliberate interruption to blocky sans or restrained systems.
9. When mixing typefaces, make the contrast structural: heavy sans versus high-contrast serif, block uppercase versus thin script, pale ghost lines versus one dark focal line.
10. Use two or three type voices maximum.
11. Keep display letterforms crisp, sharp-edged, and high-definition. Do not blur, bevel, emboss, distress, or texture main type by default.
12. Use tight leading for oversized stacks. Display text must behave as one dense graphic unit.
13. Sculpt display blocks through line breaks, indentation, staggered starts, cropping, or frame pressure.
14. Use display-scale punctuation as a graphic beat. A period, slash, bracket, or similar mark may act as mass or rhythm.
15. Use low-contrast background lettering only when it is chromatically related to the field and subordinate to foreground content.
16. For ghost typography, keep size and alignment consistent across repeated lines. Change value to identify the focal line.
17. Set microtype in uppercase, compact, tracked, monospaced-feeling, or metadata-like styling.
18. Place microtype near edges, corners, seams, lower baselines, panel footers, or tucked gaps.
19. Never let micro-labels compete with the display mass.
20. Use slash separators, dots, small circles, dotted rules, tiny squares, and hairline strokes as precise calibration marks.
21. Treat pills and chips as small spatial anchors or labels, not as buttons unless the requested medium requires buttons.
22. Use rotated vertical type as an edge spine only when it strengthens the perimeter system.
23. Use circular stamps or seals lightly as counterweights to heavier type or collage. Do not make them the focal point unless the whole layout is an emblem system.
24. Use double outline contours only for typographic emblems. Keep both outlines thin, close together, and subordinate to the packed type inside.
25. If using an expressive hand-shaped mark, make it one dominant mark with crisp opaque edges. Let the background show through internal gaps if present.
26. Do not use decorative swashes, ornamental frames, distressed lettering, or vintage flourishes unless explicitly requested and kept secondary to the scale logic.
27. Do not turn labels into UI badges, filters, buttons, or calls to action unless the requested medium requires interface states.

## Color and value

1. Limit non-photographic color to 2–4 colors.
2. Use one supported palette structure by default:
  - warm off-white or cream ground with dark brown, espresso, charcoal, or near-black marks;
  - achromatic black/white/pale gray with one dominant value contrast;
  - matte charcoal or near-black field with pale off-white type;
  - saturated single-color field with black or near-black foreground marks;
  - warm blush, peach, taupe, amber, or cream system with espresso or umber typography;
  - dark atmospheric red-black field with sharp pale type and a limited cool accent;
  - muted earth or desaturated accent panels inside a warm neutral system;
  - full-bleed luminous abstract field with compressed spectral edges and crisp pale or dark type.
3. Avoid pure white in sparse editorial compositions unless stark binary contrast is the point. Prefer warm off-white, cream, pale neutral, or paper-like grounds.
4. Use pure black only for maximum binary contrast: black type on a saturated field or hard typographic marks on white.
5. Use deep espresso instead of pure black on blush, peach, cream, or warm matte grounds when warm contrast is required.
6. Use near-black charcoal instead of glossy absolute black for dark editorial surfaces.
7. Use pale off-white instead of stark white type on charcoal when contrast must remain legible but softened.
8. Use one saturated accent as a single controlled event: one large typographic line, one small pill, one punctuation cluster, or one contained panel.
9. If using fluorescent or highly saturated color, commit almost the entire surface to it or isolate it as one graphic event. Do not sprinkle it.
10. Use black type on saturated single-color fields.
11. Keep secondary colors spatially contained inside image fragments, muted panels, or inserted shapes unless the whole concept is a chromatic field.
12. Do not use broad rainbow palettes as decoration. Spectral color must stay compressed into thin edges, ridges, seams, contour bands, or refractive fringes.
13. Use atmospheric gradients only as soft depth fields, especially dark cinematic or full-bleed abstract compositions.
14. Do not add gradients to flat matte editorial fields unless the mode explicitly includes diffuse bloom or atmospheric image.
15. Make atmospheric blooms large, soft-edged, low-saturation, radial or cloud-like, and fading into the ground without a hard contour.
16. Pair soft diffuse color forms with crisp opaque marks.
17. Keep warm editorial palettes warm: cream, blush, peach, taupe, amber, mauve, umber, espresso, muted green, warm gray.
18. Keep achromatic layouts disciplined: black, white/off-white, charcoal, and pale gray only, with pale gray subordinate.
19. Use value contrast as hierarchy. Do not use multicolor coding unless the user explicitly requests data or interface states.

## Imagery, shape, texture, and material

1. Treat imagery as graphic material: texture, cropped atmosphere, tonal mass, abstract surface, color field, or tactile interruption.
2. Do not use narrative hero photography by default.
3. Crop photographs close so they read as material, surface, fragment, or atmosphere rather than literal scene.
4. Localize photographic texture: one contained image, one image band, one constellation cluster, one lower geometric field, or one full-bleed atmospheric surface.
5. Use rectangular photographic crops without borders, shadows, or frames by default.
6. For constellations, vary image size, lighting, crop, color temperature, and texture while unifying fragments through hard-edged rectangular format.
7. Do not force varied photo fragments into one uniform filter unless the whole system is explicitly monochrome or warm grayscale.
8. Use muted or warm grayscale photography when the surrounding palette is achromatic or near-monochrome.
9. Use warm photographic bands with glow, shadowed seams, vertical divisions, and partial forms only when paired with warm matte typographic fields.
10. Use blurred abstract image panels for color-field atmosphere, not literal depiction.
11. Use full-bleed abstract fields only when the image surface is immersive. Place type and micro-marks at perimeter zones, lower thirds, or protected focal areas.
12. Use concentric rectangular bands, nested value steps, repeated curved contour bands, troughs, or refractive edges for abstract relief fields.
13. Keep spectral hues thin and edge-bound: ridgelines, contour edges, optical bands, or seams.
14. Use flat geometric panels with crisp edges to create collage depth through overlap and occlusion.
15. Use image overlap, masking, wedge cuts, jagged bites, angular cropping, and shallow occlusion instead of cast shadows.
16. Use rounded rectangles and pill forms only as part of a repeated radius system. Larger outer containers may pair with smaller-radius nested tiles.
17. Keep rounded corners restrained. Do not make components bubbly.
18. Use soft diffuse shadows only beneath floating inner tiles when slight lift is required. Keep shadows subtle and localized.
19. Avoid shadows elsewhere. Create depth with overlap, value steps, cropping, stacking, translucency, blur, or tonal contrast.
20. Do not add drop shadows to labels, photos, cards, or collage pieces.
21. Use flat matte fills, crisp vector edges, print-like surfaces, and clean panel cuts as the default material language.
22. Use subtle grain or noise only to soften gradients, luminous fields, or analog photographic texture. Do not texture every surface.
23. Keep large color fields uninterrupted by decorative surface texture.
24. Use one organic or gestural element sparingly: calligraphic mark, hand-drawn illustration, irregular linework, organic cutout, or heart/circular-like photo shape.
25. If using hand-drawn illustration, mix thin contour lines with thicker filled strokes, wobbly curves, motion arcs, and open internal gaps.
26. Preserve irregularity in hand-drawn marks. Do not smooth them into generic icons.
27. Keep surrounding typography and symbols smaller, quieter, and more controlled than the expressive mark.
28. Do not use glossy UI chrome, glassmorphism, bevels, metallic surfaces, marble, gold foil, 3D extrusion, neumorphism, skeuomorphic material cues, or glossy reflections.

## Content and subject treatment

1. Carry the aesthetic through visible structure: layout, scale, type, spacing, palette, image treatment, mark placement, and material behavior.
2. Do not depend on a specific person, product, room, plant, building, device, food item, landscape, artwork, industry, identity, or cultural scene unless the user requests it.
3. If using photography, prioritize crop, texture, tonal mass, edge behavior, and placement over narrative clarity.
4. Do not treat names, dates, roles, organizations, product categories, slogans, ratings, or metadata as required aesthetic ingredients.
5. Use generic copy only when needed for layout demonstration. Preserve display scale, microtype scale, tracking, alignment, line breaks, and density.
6. Keep labels, pills, stamps, seals, stars, dots, glyphs, and tiny icons functioning as visual marks or calibration points, not semantic UI controls by default.
7. Do not infer portfolio, agency, publication, fashion, technology, hospitality, research, museum, product, dashboard, or landing-page meaning from this visual system.
8. Do not use literal cultural or linguistic meaning of calligraphic marks as the style basis. Preserve only the visual role: flat, crisp, gestural, irregular, singular.
9. Do not preserve exact wording, names, dates, coordinates, brand colors, or interface labels unless the user supplies them and asks to keep them.
10. Keep copy blocks compact. Supporting text must be short, quiet, tracked, and spatially placed.
11. If the user requests a domain-specific design, apply this grammar through layout, typography, palette, and image treatment rather than stereotype imagery.
12. Abstract subject matter through crop, blur, masking, tonal compression, or partial occlusion when literal readability would overpower the composition.

## Medium-specific rules

### Posters, covers, key visuals, and editorial graphics

1. Use oversized display type as the main mass.
2. Use microtype as perimeter notation or tucked support.
3. Use one large field color or image surface. Do not build a busy multi-section layout.
4. Let type crop, press to edges, stagger, or stack tightly.
5. Use a single image card, collage band, abstract field, or constellation cluster as the secondary register.
6. Preserve far-distance readability through one dominant shape and a clear value relationship.
7. Use hairlines, dots, small labels, and punctuation as rhythm and calibration.

### Brand assets and typographic systems

1. Build identity through scale contrast, spacing, mark placement, and palette discipline.
2. Do not rely on logos, icons, or decorative effects as the main identity move.
3. Use one dominant type personality and one optional contrasting voice.
4. Keep secondary marks minimal: dots, slashes, micro-labels, tiny squares, thin seals, or a single accent pill.
5. Use flat vector finish and crisp edges.
6. Avoid metallic, embossed, glossy, marble, gold, or generic luxury treatments.

### Documents, reports, editorial pages, and presentation covers

1. Use large type zones paired with compact annotation zones.
2. Use warm paper-like grounds, cream fields, charcoal surfaces, or narrow achromatic palettes.
3. Keep rules hairline-thin and structural.
4. Use image material as a controlled insert, band, or abstract texture zone.
5. Avoid paragraph-heavy layouts unless the user explicitly requests a text document. Even then, preserve one dominant display register.

### Interfaces and product pages

1. Do not default to generic website or app conventions.
2. Apply this grammar through large fields, oversized type, sparse micro-labels, disciplined panels, and active negative space.
3. Avoid full-width nav bars, browser chrome, dashboards, feature-card grids, testimonial blocks, and CTA-heavy sections unless explicitly requested.
4. If buttons, pills, chips, or bars are required, keep them visually small, flat, matte, and integrated as marks or labels.
5. Use cards only for rounded panels, floating inner tiles, or flat collage modules. Do not use cards to structure every grouping.
6. Use shadows only under floating inner tiles and only as soft, diffuse, subtle lift.
7. For hover, active, or status states, use value shift, underline, hairline, or one contained accent. Do not create multicolor status systems unless requested.
8. Preserve large uninterrupted fields. Do not fill the screen with controls.

### Image-led layouts, galleries, and collages

1. Use irregular constellation placement or a controlled collage band instead of a uniform grid.
2. Keep photo crops hard-edged and borderless by default.
3. Allow some image fragments to crop off the viewport.
4. Use varied image sizes: tiny fragments, medium interruptions, and at most one larger secondary anchor.
5. Do not turn imagery into a single narrative hero unless requested.
6. Use overlap, masking, and occlusion for depth. Avoid heavy shadows or bevels.

### Illustrations and abstract visuals

1. Use flat crisp geometry, expressive linework, or luminous abstract relief fields. Do not combine all three without a clear hierarchy.
2. For gestural illustration, use one dominant irregular mark with visible contour variation.
3. For abstract luminous fields, use soft gradients, blur, refractive edges, nested bands, or contour ridges while keeping type crisp.
4. For geometric systems, use hard cuts, nested rectangles, bands, seams, and value steps.
5. Keep texture localized and low intensity unless it is inside the image field itself.

## Forbidden shortcuts

1. Do not use vague taste commands such as beautiful, premium, elevated, sleek, or modern as design logic. Convert them into visible constraints.
2. Do not use generic luxury cues: gold foil, marble, metallic gradients, embossed marks, serif-only branding, ornamental borders, or glossy black surfaces.
3. Do not add glassmorphism, neumorphism, bevels, glossy reflections, 3D extrusion, skeuomorphic depth, or heavy glow as default finish.
4. Do not add drop shadows to make cards, labels, chips, or photos stand out.
5. Do not add browser chrome, app controls, dashboard widgets, search bars, login buttons, pricing modules, or interface furniture unless specifically requested.
6. Do not turn micro-labels into large badges, buttons, filters, or functional UI tags.
7. Do not use uniform card grids, masonry grids, or evenly spaced galleries when irregular constellation or collage behavior is required.
8. Do not add borders around photographs by default.
9. Do not over-filter all photos into one branded treatment when variation is part of the visual language.
10. Do not make photos narrative hero images when their role should be crop, texture, atmosphere, fragment, or interruption.
11. Do not fill empty fields with icons, decorative blobs, patterns, extra copy, background shapes, or filler modules.
12. Do not center every layout. Use asymmetry, edge pressure, stagger, perimeter marks, split footers, and off-center clusters.
13. Do not make all text similar in size or weight.
14. Do not introduce many medium-sized headings or paragraph blocks that blur the hierarchy.
15. Do not scatter multiple saturated accents across unrelated elements.
16. Do not use rainbow color as broad stripes, fills, or general decoration. Keep spectral color on thin edges or refractive seams.
17. Do not make warm editorial palettes cold white and blue.
18. Do not turn matte charcoal layouts into glossy black tech screens.
19. Do not apply gradients to every background. Use gradients only for atmospheric fields, diffuse blooms, or luminous abstract surfaces.
20. Do not use decorative full-canvas frames. Structure the perimeter with tiny marks, rules, type, or cropped elements.
21. Do not use distressed vintage texture in clean monochrome typographic or emblem layouts.
22. Do not make rounded components look like generic mobile UI buttons. Rounding must belong to a broader panel, tile, or pill grammar.
23. Do not overuse hand-drawn irregularity. One expressive mark is enough unless the user requests an illustration-led composition.
24. Do not rely on literal image subject, names, industries, identities, or brand categories to create the aesthetic.
25. Do not impose symmetry where uneven density, diagonal tension, or off-center balance is required.
26. Do not add decorative linework everywhere. Hairlines and dotted rules must calibrate or separate.
27. Do not treat empty space as a problem. Empty space is a main structural material.

## Generation checklist

Before finishing, verify:

1. There is one dominant visual mass: oversized type, image field, collage cluster, rounded panel pair, packed emblem, atmospheric field, or expressive mark.
2. Hierarchy uses extreme scale contrast instead of many medium sizes.
3. Display type is at least 8× taller than microtype, preferably 15× or more in typographic layouts.
4. There are only 2–4 visible type size levels.
5. Large type behaves as architecture, mass, edge pressure, or graphic shape.
6. Micro-labels are tiny, tracked/uppercase or metadata-like, and placed at edges, seams, corners, footers, or gaps.
7. The layout is asymmetrical unless a centered emblem, protected headline, or diptych structure is intentional.
8. Dense zones are concentrated instead of evenly spread.
9. Negative space is active, protected, and free of filler decoration.
10. The palette is narrow: one ground, one primary mark color, and at most one accent or muted secondary family.
11. Any saturated color is either the whole field or one controlled event.
12. Warm systems stay warm: cream, blush, taupe, amber, mauve, umber, espresso, charcoal, muted green, or warm gray.
13. Gradients appear only as atmospheric blooms or luminous fields.
14. Typography, marks, and rules remain crisp against soft imagery or atmospheric color.
15. Photos are cropped, fragmented, abstracted, or contained rather than used as generic narrative hero imagery.
16. Photo fragments are borderless and shadowless unless a rounded tile system explicitly requires containment.
17. Depth comes from overlap, occlusion, cropping, value, blur, translucency, or stacking rather than heavy shadows or bevels.
18. Rounded corners, if used, are restrained and consistent.
19. Hairlines read as datums or calibration marks, not heavy dividers.
20. Any expressive script, serif, calligraphic mark, or illustration is singular and controlled.
21. All small marks serve a compositional role.
22. Generic UI chrome, browser controls, nav bars, dashboards, and card grids are absent unless specifically requested.
23. Gold, marble, metallics, glassmorphism, glossy reflections, bevels, and unsupported luxury or tech shortcuts are absent.
24. The composition reads as one unified graphic field, not a collection of unrelated modules.
25. Content specifics remain incidental; the aesthetic is carried by structure, type, color, image treatment, material behavior, and spacing.
